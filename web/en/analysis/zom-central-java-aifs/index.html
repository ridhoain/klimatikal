How well does an AI weather model forecast ten-day rainfall in the seasonal zones of Central Java?

Ainur Ridho · Klimatikal · Data through the ten-day period starting 21 October 2026

Abstract

AI-based weather models are now openly available, but it is not clear how reliable they are for rainfall at the scale of seasonal zones in Indonesia. We tested ECMWF AIFS Single, one public AI model, on ten-day (dasarian) rainfall totals in the 54 BMKG seasonal zones (ZOM) that overlap Central Java. Forecasts were compared with CHIRPS v3.0 observations and with the 1991–2020 CHIRPS climatology, over 60 ten-day periods (March 2025–October 2026) that all lie outside the model's training data. Averaged over the 10 zones that are large enough for the model grid, the model's mean absolute error was – lower than climatology at a 0-day lead (95% interval: ) and – lower at a 5-day lead (). The advantage is not even through the year: – in the dry season (Jun–Sep) but only – in the wet season (Dec–Mar), with an interval that includes zero (). The sample is short and the model version changed during the period, so the results are preliminary.

1. Introduction

In Indonesia, farming and water management depend heavily on rainfall, and BMKG divides the country into seasonal zones (ZOM), each with its own wet and dry season pattern [8]. Machine-learning weather models, trained on decades of reanalysis data, now produce global forecasts in minutes, and some are released openly, including ECMWF's AIFS [3]. Such models are usually judged with global measures such as geopotential height or temperature. Rainfall is harder: it is intermittent, very local, and a model with about 25 km resolution cannot resolve small zones.

This paper asks one narrow question: measured against the CHIRPS satellite-gauge product, are AIFS ten-day rainfall totals closer to what happened than the climate average (climatology) in the seasonal zones of Central Java, and how quickly does that advantage fade when the forecast is issued earlier? The scope is deliberately limited to forecasts up to 15 days. Seasonal forecasts and rainy-season onset are not tested because they lie beyond the model's range, and this paper neither compares nor scores the operational forecasts of any agency, including BMKG.

2. Data and methods

2.1 Study area

ZOM boundaries and numbers come from BMKG's public map service [8]. We use the 54 zones that overlap Central Java and compute their areas ourselves (UTM zone 49S). The main average uses the 10 zones with at least 28 land CHIRPS pixels (about 850 km², more than one model cell) and at least 80% of their area inside the province. The other zones are still shown, with a warning, but are not used for conclusions.

2.2 Observations

As the reference we use CHIRPS v3.0 (final) [1][2], a rainfall product that blends satellite imagery with gauge data at 0.05° (about 5 km). Ten-day totals are computed for days 1–10, 11–20 and 21–month end, then averaged per zone with land-pixel area weights (5×5 sub-sampling per pixel). CHIRPS is an estimate, not a direct measurement; in our check of 1991–2020 over Java, CHIRPS v3.0 was on average about 11% wetter than v2.0.

2.3 Forecasts

AIFS Single is a global neural-network weather model from ECMWF with 0.25° resolution and forecasts to 15 days [3][4]. We took the 00 UTC forecasts from ECMWF open data [6]. A ten-day total is the difference in accumulated precipitation (tp) between the end and the start of the period. Lead is the number of days between when the forecast was issued and the start of the period; because the whole period must fit within 15 days, lead is limited to 0–5 days. The model field is interpolated bilinearly to CHIRPS pixels and averaged with the same weights as the observations.

The scored period is 1 March 2025 to 31 October 2026 (60 ten-day periods). AIFS was trained on ERA5 1979–2022 [5] and fine-tuned on operational analyses 2016–2022 [7][4], so the whole period lies outside the training data. Version 1.1.0 was released on 27 August 2025 to correct a precipitation issue in the first version [4]; results are therefore also split by the date the forecast was issued.

2.4 Climatology, anomaly and verification measures

Climatology is the 1991–2020 CHIRPS mean for the same dasarian of the year (36 per year), and the anomaly is the value minus climatology. We use three measures. MAE skill = 1 − (forecast mean absolute error ÷ climatology mean absolute error): zero means the same as climatology, one is perfect, negative is worse [10][11]. Anomaly correlation measures whether the forecast raises and lowers rainfall from normal at the same times as reality. Bias is the mean forecast − observation (mm per ten days). 95% confidence intervals come from a circular block bootstrap (blocks of 3 periods, 1,000 resamples) so that correlation between consecutive periods is respected [9].

2.5 Map presentation

The map uses the 0.05° CHIRPS grid and shows CHIRPS, climatology, anomaly, the AIFS forecast and its error (AIFS − CHIRPS). The AIFS field is shown after interpolation to the same grid, so pixel detail comes from interpolation, not from the model, whose cells are 0.25°. The sea is masked with the Klimatikal base-map coastline; CHIRPS coastal pixels are extended by up to three pixels to reach the coastline. Map data are 8-bit coded on a square-root scale (quantisation error up to about 4 mm), and ZOM boundaries are simplified and clipped at the coastline.

3.5 Differences between zones

Skill by zone (the "Skill by zone" layer in Figure 1) varies, and small zones or zones partly outside the province show less stable values. ZOM 147 lies only about 62% inside Central Java and is scored separately; ZOM 107 (about 176 km², 6 CHIRPS pixels) is smaller than one model cell (0.25° ≈ 700 km²) so its result is not used. Because each zone uses the same 60-period sample as the average, per-zone intervals are wider than the interval for the average, and differences between zones should not be read as real without looking at the intervals.

4. Conclusions

Over 60 ten-day periods outside the training data, AIFS Single ten-day rainfall totals were closer to CHIRPS than climatology in the larger seasonal zones of Central Java, at every lead from 0 to 5 days. The advantage comes mainly from the dry season; in the wet season, when rainfall matters most, the advantage is not demonstrated by the available data. These findings are early indications, not a complete test, because of the following limitations:

  • The period is only twenty months, about one and a half wet seasons, and the model version changed in the middle (18 and 42 periods at lead 0 before and after 27 August 2025).
  • The benchmark is climatology only. We have not tested a stronger benchmark such as the previous period's anomaly.
  • The reference is CHIRPS, an estimate, and a 0.25° model cannot resolve the detail of small zones.
  • Only one model and one region were tested, so the results should not be generalised to other AI models or regions.
  • Rainy-season onset and seasonal forecasts are not tested because they lie beyond the model's 15-day range.
